1. The project in one page

The idea

The group's question is: how can robots assist or enhance humans in design? Instead of the robot being a labourer, it becomes a creative partner. Our concrete experience: a visitor puts on a headset, designs a small wall by drawing a curve on a real table, sees the wall appear in front of them in augmented reality, watches a see-through "ghost" robot build it in the headset, confirms, and then the real UR5 robot builds it with real timber bricks. Afterwards a camera-based system (Suzanne) lets the robot take the wall apart again, so the next visitor can start fresh.

Who does what

PersonPart

Matthias NahumThe robot side: making the UR5 move bricks. His route goes through Grasshopper (inside Rhino).
Suzanne MinkelsComputer vision: a Raspberry Pi camera reads markers on the table and bricks, so the robot can take the wall apart.

The lecturer's key requirement

The user must see the robot build the wall in the headset and confirm, before the real robot moves. The lecturer pointed us to HANDZONe, a TU Delft system that shows a live robot in VR. We took HANDZONe's 3D models of the UR5 and its table, and built our own preview around them.

Where we are on 21 October

  • ✅ The headset shows the real room (passthrough) with virtual bricks, table and robot lined up on a real table.
  • ✅ You place the table with two touches, draw a curve, choose 1–3 layers, and six bricks immediately form a wall along the curve (preview).
  • ✅ Press A to confirm; the wall is sent over Wi-Fi to the laptop.
  • ✅ The laptop works out every joint angle the robot needs (our own robot maths), and sends the movements back.
  • ✅ The ghost robot in the headset picks each brick from the tray and builds the wall, exactly as the real robot would.
  • ⏳ Not yet: running it on the real UR5 at the LAMA table (needs a lab session with Matthias).
The whole thing in one comparison. Think of an architect, a messenger and a builder. The headset is the architect's sketchbook: you draw, and it turns your sketch into an exact list of brick positions. The laptop is the messenger plus the builder's foreman: it receives the list and works out every move the builder's arm must make. The robot is the builder. Before the builder lifts anything, the foreman sends a rehearsal back to the sketchbook: a see-through robot acts out the build, so you can say "yes, go" or "no, let me redraw".

2. The words you need

Grouped by topic. You don't need to memorise these; come back when a word is unclear.

Reality: VR, AR and passthrough

WordMeaning
VR (virtual reality)You see only a computer-made world; the real room is hidden.
AR (augmented reality)You see the real world with computer-made objects added on top.
MR (mixed reality)Often used for AR where virtual things respond to the real room. Our app is AR/MR.
XR (extended reality)Umbrella word for VR, AR and MR. Unity's tools for headsets are called "XR".
Passthrough (video see-through, VST)The headset's outside cameras film the room and show it on the screens inside, live. That is how a VR headset becomes an AR headset. The Pico calls it "Video Seethrough".
TrackingThe headset constantly measures where it and the controllers are in the room (position and rotation), using its cameras and motion sensors.
CalibrationTelling the app where a real thing is. Our app doesn't know where your table is until you touch it with the controller.
Rig / XR OriginThe invisible "you" in the app: the camera (your eyes) plus the two controllers (your hands).
Ghost robotOur see-through 3D model of the UR5 that acts out the build in the headset before the real robot moves.

Programming and apps

WordMeaning
Code / scriptWritten instructions for the computer. Our app's scripts are in the language C# ("C sharp"); the laptop bridge is in Python.
CompileTranslate human-readable code into something the machine runs. "Compile error" = the translator found a mistake and stops.
BuildPackage the whole project into one installable app.
APKThe installable app file for Android (the Pico runs Android). Like an .exe on Windows.
Sign / keystoreAndroid only installs apps that carry a digital signature. The signature comes from a private file, the keystore (user.keystore), protected by a password. It's like a wax seal: only the owner of the seal can make updates to the same app. Never upload it publicly.
SDK (software development kit)A toolbox a company gives developers so their apps can use its hardware. The PICO SDK lets Unity talk to the Pico headset (passthrough, controllers, tracking).
PackageA ready-made add-on for Unity (for example the PICO SDK, or the XR Interaction Toolkit for grabbing things).
Batch modeRunning Unity without its window, from a command, to check that everything compiles or to run a setup step automatically.
Log / consoleThe running diary of a program: messages, warnings (yellow) and errors (red).
BugA mistake in a program that makes it behave wrongly. Debugging = finding and fixing it.

Unity words

WordMeaning
Unity / Unity EditorA "game engine": a program for building interactive 3D apps and games. The Editor is the window where you build the project.
Unity HubThe launcher that installs Unity versions and opens projects. We use exactly version 6000.3.8f1, the same as HANDZONe.
ProjectThe folder with everything for one app (our project folder is PicoBricks).
SceneOne "stage" in the app with everything on it (our scene: SampleScene.unity).
GameObjectAny thing in the scene: a brick, the table, the camera, an invisible helper.
ComponentA piece of behaviour or property attached to a GameObject: its shape (mesh), its material, physics (Rigidbody), or one of our scripts.
TransformEvery GameObject's position, rotation and scale. Transforms can be nested: a child moves with its parent.
PrefabA saved, reusable GameObject (like a template). The HANDZONe robot + table came in as a prefab.
AssetAny file the project uses: models, materials, scripts, sounds.
Mesh / materialMesh = the 3D shape (triangles). Material = how it looks (colour, transparency).
Rigidbody / kinematicRigidbody = the object obeys physics (gravity, collisions). Kinematic = physics switched off; the object stays exactly where the code puts it.
ColliderAn invisible shape used for bumping into things.
URPUniversal Render Pipeline: Unity's way of drawing images, tuned for mobile devices like the Pico.
HDRHigh dynamic range colour. In our case it caused a black background, because its colour format had no transparency (see Part 8).
Play modeRunning the app inside the Editor on the laptop, without the headset.
Build and Run (Ctrl+B)Build the APK and install it on the connected headset.

Sharing work: Git and GitHub

WordMeaning
GitA program that keeps the full history of a project folder, so you can go back to any earlier version and work in parallel safely.
GitHubA website that stores Git projects online, so a team can share them. Ours: github.com/jintecture/PicoBricks (private).
Repository (repo)One project tracked by Git, with all its history.
CommitA saved snapshot of the project with a short message ("what changed and why"). Like a page in a diary.
Push / pullPush = upload your new commits to GitHub. Pull = download your teammates' commits.
CloneDownload a full copy of a repository for the first time.
BranchA parallel line of work. You can try something on a branch without disturbing the main version (main).
MergeCombine a branch back into another. A merge conflict happens when two people changed the same lines; a human picks the right version.
Pull request (PR)On GitHub, a request "please merge my branch", where others can look first.
TagA permanent bookmark on one commit, like v1-manual-placement.
.gitignoreA list of files Git should not track (huge generated folders, the private keystore, passwords).
Git LFSLarge File Storage: an add-on for big files (HANDZONe uses it, 6 GB).
Git in one comparison. Git is a Google Docs version history for a whole folder, but with deliberate "save points" (commits) that carry a note, and with the ability to make a parallel copy (branch) to try an idea and later fold it back in (merge).

Robots

WordMeaning
UR5A robot arm by Universal Robots. 6 rotating joints, reach 850 mm, carries up to 5 kg. Ours is an older "CB3" model.
JointA motor that rotates one part of the arm. The six joints in order: base (turns the whole arm), shoulder, elbow, wrist 1, wrist 2, wrist 3.
Joint anglesSix numbers (degrees or radians) that fully describe the arm's pose, for example (0, −90, −90, −90, 90, 0).
FlangeThe round metal plate at the end of the arm, where a tool is bolted on.
Tool / gripper / TCPThe gripper is the "hand" bolted to the flange. TCP (tool centre point) is the exact point of the tool that should go to a target, for example between the gripper's fingertips.
Forward kinematics (FK)From joint angles to "where is the hand?". Easy: just follow the arm link by link.
Inverse kinematics (IK)From "I want the hand here, pointing down" to "which joint angles?". Harder; for the UR5 there is a known exact formula with up to 8 answers (elbow up/down, wrist flipped, etc.).
ReachHow far from its base the robot can put its tool. 850 mm is the maximum; with the gripper pointing down it's less.
Teach pendant / PolyscopeThe robot's tablet with buttons, and its software. You can read coordinates there and move ("jog") the arm by hand.
URScriptThe UR robot's own little programming language (for example movej = move by joints, movel = move in a straight line).
MoveJ / MoveLMoveJ: each joint turns smoothly to its new angle (the hand moves in a curve). MoveL: the hand moves in a straight line (used for going straight down to a brick).
RoboDKSoftware that simulates robots on the laptop and can drive real ones. Its trial licence on Jin's laptop expired on 19 October.
URSimUniversal Robots' simulator of the robot's controller.
Digital twinA virtual copy of a real machine that mirrors it. Our ghost robot is a preview twin.

Geometry and coordinates

WordMeaning
Coordinate frameA starting point plus three directions (x, y, z) you measure from. The same point has different numbers in different frames, like an address given "from the station" or "from the church".
Table frame (our "Workspace")Our main frame. Origin = the middle of the table's front edge (where you stand), on the table top. x = to your right, "away" = into the table, and up.
Robot base frameThe robot's own frame, starting at the centre of its base, in millimetres.
y-up / z-upUnity calls "up" y; robots, Rhino and most engineers call "up" z. Converting means swapping axes.
Left-handed / right-handedTwo mirror-image conventions for which way the third axis points. Unity is left-handed; robots are right-handed. This flips the sign of rotations.
YawRotation around the vertical axis ("which way does the brick point, seen from above").
Degrees / radiansTwo units for angles: 180° = π radians ≈ 3.14. Robots often use radians.
Bounding boxThe smallest box around an object. We measured the LAMA table model this way.

Networks

WordMeaning
IP addressA device's address on a network, for example 192.168.0.150 (headset at home), 192.168.0.249 (laptop at home).
PortA numbered "door" on a device, so different programs can receive their own messages. We use port 5005 (headset → laptop) and 5006 (laptop → headset).
UDPA way of sending short messages without a handshake, like posting a postcard. Fast, but a postcard can get lost.
TCPA way of sending with a handshake and delivery check, like a phone call. (Matthias's Grasshopper link uses TCP.)
BroadcastSending a message to every device on the local network at once, in case the exact address is wrong.
JSONA plain-text format for structured data, with labels and values: {"x": 0.12, "yaw": -90}. Readable by people and by every programming language.
HotspotThe laptop makes its own Wi-Fi network. Windows gives the laptop the address 192.168.137.1 there. At home we don't need it.
adbAndroid Debug Bridge: a command-line tool to talk to an Android device over USB (install apps, read logs, copy files). Unity uses it to install our app on the Pico.
USB debugging / developer modeHeadset settings that allow a computer to install and debug apps over the cable.

5. How one wall travels through the whole system

This part follows one wall from the laptop where the app is made, into the headset, over Wi-Fi to the laptop, back to the headset, and (soon) to the robot. At every step: what goes in, what comes out, and what is converted.

Step 0. Making the app (laptop → headset, by USB)

InWhat happensOut

Then Ctrl+B: Unity compiles the C# code into machine code for the headset's ARM processor (IL2CPP), packs everything, and signs it with the keystore.An app file PicoBricks.apk (~60 MB)

Step 1. Placing the table (inside the headset)

Why: when the app starts, it has no idea where your real table is. Calibration tells it.

  1. Press B. A white ball sits 6 cm in front of the right controller: the "measuring tip".
  2. Touch the table's front-right corner (next to the robot), pull the trigger. The app records that 3D point.
  3. Touch any point further left on the front edge, pull the trigger.

Conversion: the two points give a direction ("right" along the front edge). "Away from you" is worked out as the direction at a right angle to it, flat on the table. The app knows the LAMA table's real size (143 × 61 cm, 91 cm high), so from the right corner it places the whole table: its front-edge middle becomes the origin of the table frame (the "Workspace"). Everything else (the robot, the tray, the bricks, the drawing area) is a child of this Workspace, so it all moves into place together.

Analogy. Pinning a paper map onto a real field. Two pins are enough: the first fixes where the map is, the second fixes which way it's turned. Once pinned, everything printed on the map (robot, tray, wall area) lands in the right spot in the field.

(Without the ghost robot, an older 4-point version measures any table: front-left, front-right, back-left corner and the floor.)

Step 2. Drawing the curve (inside the headset)

  • Input: you point the right controller's laser at the table and hold the trigger.
  • The app finds where the laser ray crosses the table plane ("ray–plane intersection"), converts that point into table coordinates, keeps it inside the black outline, and stores a point every ~1 cm.
  • When you release, the curve is smoothed: each point is averaged with its neighbours a few times, to remove hand wobble (the first and last point stay).
  • Output: a list of points in table coordinates (metres), shown as a cyan line.

Step 3. From a curve to a wall: the brick logic (inside the headset)

  1. Resize. One layer of bricks has a fixed length: 6, 3 or 2 bricks × 10 cm. The curve is scaled around its middle until it is exactly that long. A quick small sketch and a big sweep give the same shape at wall size.
  2. Dividers. From where a brick starts, the app looks for the point on the curve exactly 10 cm away (a circle of 10 cm around the start, crossing the curve). The brick points there. The next brick starts where this one ends.
  3. Folding ruler. Two straight bricks can't both follow a bend: one side opens a gap, the other would overlap. So neighbouring bricks share the corner on the inside of the bend, like the hinge of a folding ruler. Since 21 October the app tries both possible hinge corners for every brick, checks the candidate against all bricks laid so far (an exact overlap test, the "separating axis test"), and keeps the one that lands closest to the curve. If a bend is too sharp for 10 cm bricks, it is straightened just enough.
  4. Stack. Every layer repeats the same chain, straight up (layer height 5 cm).
  5. Fit into the robot's area. Every corner, edge midpoint and centre of the bottom bricks must be on the table, off the tray, and 20–75 cm from the robot's base. If not, the app tries sliding the whole wall (same shape) up to 40 cm in 1 cm steps and takes the smallest move that fits.

Output: a plan: for each brick, its centre (x, y, z in metres in the table frame), its rotation, and its footprint. The bricks immediately stand there as a preview. The thumbstick changes 1/2/3 layers and the preview updates. Trigger = draw again.

Step 4. Confirm, and send to the laptop (headset → laptop, Wi-Fi, port 5005)

Press A = "I'm happy with this wall". Then:

  1. WallBuilder saves the plan on the headset as wall_plan.json and announces "confirmed".
  2. BrickSender builds a message and sends it twice: straight to the laptop's address, and as a broadcast (in case the address is wrong). It's one UDP packet of about 1.5 KB.

Conversion inside BrickSender (Unity → robot-style axes): Unity uses x right, y up, z away, and measures yaw clockwise. The message uses x right, y away, z up, and yaw counter-clockwise, like robots and Rhino. So y and z are swapped and the yaw's sign is flipped. Yaw 0 means the brick's long side points away from you.

{ "type": "layout", "seq": 3, "frame": "table", "units": "m_deg",
  "bricks": [
    { "id": 1, "name": "Brick_1", "standing": false,
      "pick":  { "x": 0.1155, "y": 0.3915, "z": 0.025, "yaw": 0.0 },     ← its pocket in the tray
      "place": { "x": -0.150, "y": 0.120,  "z": 0.025, "yaw": -90.0 } },  ← its spot in the wall
    ...5 more... ],
  "robot": { "valid": true, "x": 0.47, "y": 0.31, "z": 0.0,             ← where the ghost robot stands
             "armYaw0": 90, "armYaw90": 180, "check": [ ... ] } }        ← for the automatic line-up check

seq is the wall's number, so the laptop can ignore the duplicate copy. The bricks are sorted bottom layer first, then along the curve: that's the build order.

Step 5. The laptop bridge converts the wall for the robot

The bridge (picobricks_bridge.py, started with PICOBRICKS.bat → option 3) waits on port 5005. When a wall arrives, it saves it as last_layout.json and converts it in several stages:

StageFrom → toHow
a. Line up with the ghost (simulation)Headset's ghost position → a "table frame" for the robotThe headset says where the ghost robot stands and which way its arm points. The bridge compares that with its own UR5 maths, and works out how the robot is turned on the table. (For the real robot, calib.json is used instead: the robot's own coordinates of the table's two front corners, read off the teach pendant.)
b. Clean upBrick positions → tidy positionsHeights are rounded to whole brick layers (the headset can be a few mm off and stacking isn't forgiving). A brick turned 180° looks the same, so the yaw is kept between −90° and 90°.
c. Table → robot basemetres in the table frame → millimetres from the robot's baseMove (subtract the base position), rotate (the robot's turn on the table), scale ×1000.
d. Brick centre → gripper targetwhere the brick is → where the gripper's tip must goThe tip goes to 25 mm below the brick's top face (grasp depth). For placing, 2 mm higher (so it releases just above). The tool points straight down, turned with the brick's yaw.
e. The routepick and place targets → a sequence of movesFor each brick: go to 250 mm above the table over the pocket → straight down (last 80 mm slowly) → close gripper → straight up → over to the wall → straight down → open → up. Then back home.
f. Gripper tip → flangetool tip → the end plate of the armThe gripper is assumed 150 mm long, so the flange is 150 mm above the tip.
g. Inverse kinematicsflange position → 6 joint anglesur5_kinematics.py solves it with the exact UR5 formula. Each pose can have up to 8 solutions (elbow up/down, wrist flipped...); it picks the one closest to the previous pose, so the arm moves smoothly instead of flipping. Straight moves are split into 4 in-between poses each.

Output: about 200 poses for 6 bricks, each a list of 6 joint angles; some poses are marked "close gripper on Brick_3" or "open". If anything can't be reached, an error message instead ("can't reach Brick_3 in the tray, 9 cm from the robot: too close to its base").

Inverse kinematics, in plain words. When you reach for a cup, your brain chooses shoulder, elbow and wrist angles without you thinking about it. Inverse kinematics is that calculation for the robot: "hand there, pointing down" in, six joint angles out. Forward kinematics is the reverse and much easier: from the angles, follow the arm piece by piece to see where the hand ends up. We use forward kinematics to check the inverse: 2000 random poses, 0.000 mm error.

About 12 KB, still one UDP packet. Sent to the address the wall came from.

Step 7. The ghost robot acts it out (inside the headset)

  1. GhostPlayer receives the poses, remembers each brick's wall spot, and puts the bricks back in the tray.
  2. It moves the ghost's joints smoothly from pose to pose (each joint turns at most 90° per second; this is "interpolation": filling in the in-between angles), like a flip-book.
  3. At "close": the brick is attached to the robot's hand and travels with it. At "open": it's released exactly on its wall spot.
  4. A label above the robot shows progress ("picking Brick_3 (3/6)"), then "Robot preview done. On the laptop: Run? [Y/n]".

Conversion here: real joint angles → the 3D model's rotations. HANDZONe's robot model is built from nested parts, each turning around its own local axis. Each joint's rotation = its starting rotation + direction (±1) × angle. Two extra corrections: the model's "zero" is upright while the real robot's zero lies flat, so the shoulder and wrist 1 get +90°.

Automatic check: the headset also sends where the ghost's hand is in 3 test poses; the bridge compares with its maths and prints "ghost robot matches the UR5 maths (within 35 mm)". The small difference is because the two measure slightly different points of the hand; a wrong joint direction would give tens of centimetres.

Step 8. The real robot (next step, not yet tested)

  • The laptop asks "Run? [Y/n]" (a second safety confirmation, and time to take the headset off).
  • Option 8 in the menu writes a URScript program and sends it to the robot (IP 192.168.1.198, port 30002) over an Ethernet cable. Option 7 uses RoboDK (needs a licence).
  • Before that, at the robot: menu 5 = jog the robot's tip onto the table's two front corners and type the coordinates (this creates calib.json); menu 6 = the robot hovers 30 mm above both corners to check.
  • Planned improvement: send the robot the same joint angles the ghost played, so it can't take a different route.

9. The rest of the team and the two robot routes

Suzanne: computer vision for taking the wall apart

A Raspberry Pi 3 with a camera on a tripod films the table and streams it to the laptop (RTSP stream via "mediamtx"). ArUco markers (square black-and-white codes) lie flat on the table and are stuck on the bricks. Her "Table Camera Toolkit" (Python) calibrates the camera with a checkerboard, finds the markers, and works out the table plane and each marker's height. Grasshopper turns that into brick positions, and the Robots plugin makes the UR5 remove the top layer; then the table is scanned again, until the wall is gone.

The project in 1 minute

"We're making a robot a design partner instead of a labourer. You put on a headset and see the real table, with a robot arm and six timber bricks. You draw a curve on the table with the controller, choose how many layers, and the bricks immediately form that wall in AR. When you press A, the wall goes over Wi-Fi to a laptop, which calculates every movement the robot arm needs and sends it back. A see-through robot in the headset then builds your wall brick by brick, exactly as the real robot will. Next, the real UR5 builds it, and a camera system lets the robot take it apart again for the next visitor."

The project in 5 minutes (talking points)

  1. Concept: human-led design, the robot as a creative partner; the user must see and confirm what the robot will do (communication between human and robot).
  2. AR, not VR: passthrough shows the real room; virtual things are lined up with the real table by touching two points.
  3. Designing: draw a curve, choose 1–3 layers; the app turns the curve into brick positions with two simple rules (dividers and folding ruler), keeps it within the robot's reach, and checks bricks never overlap.
  4. Confirming: the bricks preview the wall; A sends it.
  5. Data: a short JSON message over Wi-Fi to the laptop; the laptop converts it from table coordinates to robot coordinates and solves the robot's joint angles (inverse kinematics), about 200 poses.
  6. Rehearsal: the poses go back to the headset, where a ghost UR5 acts out the build.
  7. Real robot: after a second confirmation on the laptop, the same movements go to the UR5. The 3D-printed tray makes sure every real brick starts in a known spot.
  8. Team: Suzanne's camera system for disassembly, Matthias's Grasshopper route; the routes still have to be combined or chosen.

Questions people might ask you

QuestionShort answer
How does the headset know where the table is?It doesn't, until you touch two points on the table's front edge. From those and the known table size it places everything.
How does the robot know where the bricks are?They start in a 3D-printed tray at a fixed spot; the same tray is in the app, so every pocket's position is known.
What exactly is sent between devices?Short JSON text messages over Wi-Fi: the wall (brick pick and place positions) one way, the robot's joint angles the other way.
Why not just use RoboDK?Its licence expired, and the robot's joint maths is a known exact formula; doing it ourselves needs no licence and the preview uses exactly the robot's angles.
Why does the wall sometimes move a bit?If it pokes out of the robot's reach, the app slides it to the nearest spot that fits, keeping the shape.
Why only 6 bricks and 3 options?A deliberate first version (rectangle). It gets the whole chain working; a triangle pattern is next.
Is it safe?Two confirmations (headset and laptop), slow speeds for first tests, emergency stop in hand, and the ghost preview shows every move first.
What did you learn?Version control with Git, Unity and AR, coordinate systems, how robots are controlled (forward and inverse kinematics), and a lot of debugging: measure, check each step, change one thing at a time.
